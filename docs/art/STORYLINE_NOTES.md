# Storyline notes: the scientist in the bunker

Planning notes only. These describe the proposed narrative; they do not change the game or its current roadmap. Names, dialogue, later locations, and motives below are working suggestions.

## Core premise

A scientist caused the world's destruction through his electrical creations. As those creations overrun the surrounding world, he flees to his bunker and uses his own technology to survive.

The player initially sees a frightened person escaping a disaster. They help him reach shelter, restore power, and withstand the first attack. Only afterward do recovered evidence and his remembered responses expose his role in what happened.

The central emotional progression is sympathy -> curiosity -> suspicion -> recognition -> a changed understanding of survival. His responsibility is established; his original motives and eventual response remain open for development.

## Opening: earn sympathy through action

1. A small figure struggles down a ruined road. Behind him, electrical disturbances illuminate approaching shapes. Show scale, exhaustion, and immediate danger before showing his profession.
2. He reaches a partly buried structure. His shaking hand finds the latch with practiced familiarity. It reads as refuge now and prior ownership later.
3. The door jams. He forces it shut while something strikes the other side. A torn sleeve exposes an old electrical burn.
4. Inside, only emergency light works. He moves straight to a particular breaker without searching. The player restores a small amount of power.
5. A defense coil rises outside. Its first discharge briefly lights the attacker, but does not linger on identifying details.
6. The attack subsides. He sits against the wall beside equipment that looks recently prepared. Give the player a moment of relief before introducing ordinary production and defense.

This can be a sequence of layered illustrated panels with a few interactive actions. It does not require a separate movement game or a fully animated cinematic.

Avoid an introductory narrator naming him the destroyer. Also avoid a false factual introduction that calls him an innocent stranger. The scene can truthfully show fear while withholding the larger context.

## How evidence and memories work

Each important discovery has three parts:

- **An observable artifact:** something the player can inspect, with readable details that support the inference.
- **A source account:** a witness note, recorded message, photograph, diagram, or physical trace with a plausible origin.
- **His recollection:** a short first-person line about the specific moment, triggered when the player examines the entry.

Use concrete recollections before explanations: the sound of a switch, an argument, a damaged part, a person waiting outside. Early lines can be partial or evasive, but they should not repeatedly pretend he does not recognize his own work. He remembers; the player is learning.

Keep physical evidence, other people's claims, and his interpretation visibly distinct. His reaction need not become the codex's unquestioned account of events.

### First-region clue progression

| Discovery | What the player finds | Suggested recollection | New inference |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Bunker arrival | A fitted coat hook, a prepared workstation, and a worn path to the emergency switch | "Third switch. Even in the dark." | He knows this place intimately. |
| Perimeter claim | A broken gate with a scratched winding pattern matching the defense coil | "I rewound that one twice. It still pulled to the left." | He has worked on equipment outside the bunker. |
| Scrapyard claim | A photograph of a person resembling him beside an intact prototype; the machine's damaged counterpart lies nearby | "They asked me to stand beside it. I remember wanting them to move back." | His connection to the creations is personal. |
| Relay Grounds claim | An interrupted field recording and a switch assembly bearing his distinctive repair | "They said wait. My hand was already on the lever." | He made a consequential choice. |

The first region establishes a strong connection and responsibility for a dangerous activation. Reserve evidence of the worldwide causal chain for later discoveries so the reveal has room to develop.

### Later reveal beats

| Proposed location | Evidence | Suggested recollection | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Abandoned residential district | An evacuation message describing the scientist's assurances, beside failed household power hardware | "I told her the lights would stay on." | Give the disaster a human cost. |
| Prototype facility | His notes beside the first autonomous creation, with matching components in present enemies | "It wasn't supposed to choose its own targets." | Connect authorship to behavior. |
| Central transmission site | Independent records of his activation, warnings, and the spread through the network | "I heard the warning. I thought I had accounted for it." | Make culpability clear through corroborated evidence. |
| Return to the bunker | Preparations whose dates precede the catastrophe | "I stocked this room before I told them it was safe." | Reinterpret the opening refuge. |

These are expansion concepts, not additional requirements for the current three-sector prototype.

## Codex / memory book

Working concept: **Recovered Records**. It collects evidence and his responses rather than presenting a complete biography from the beginning.

Each entry should contain an artifact image, a neutral title, its source location, readable evidence text, and a distinct character-response area. First examination plays or displays the recollection; later examinations can replay it. Additional evidence may add a dated annotation while preserving the original response.

Suggested categories: objects, testimonies, machines, and places. Start with only the categories needed. Unfound entries should not expose spoiler titles or portraits. Do not depend on handwriting baked into an image: provide selectable text alongside it.

Sector claims and selected events award discoveries once. Routine repeatable kills should not repeat major revelations. Preserve discovered records through failed defenses and future expedition resets. Essential conclusions should also appear through main progression; optional inspection can deepen them without making the core story easy to miss.

## Visual storytelling rules

- Establish one recognizable engineering signature: for example, an asymmetrical copper winding, three ceramic insulators, and a distinctive hand-cut fastening plate.
- Repeat that signature on his gauntlet, bunker equipment, enemy interiors, and recovered diagrams. Start with subtle matches; later show exact ones.
- Let damage expose connections. An enemy's broken armor reveals the same winding the player has been upgrading.
- Show lives interrupted: an abandoned repair, a packed bag, an evacuation tally. Do not make every ruin a clue about the scientist.
- Reuse the opening composition later, with new context or newly noticed details. The same bunker door can come to mean preparation as well as safety.
- Keep the scientist frightened and human without erasing his agency. Whether he accepts responsibility, rationalizes it, or tries to repair the harm can become the later story's question.

## Decisions to resolve before final character art

- Original goal: abundant energy, autonomous labor, military deterrence, or another specific ambition.
- Failure mechanism: how his electrical creations escaped control and spread worldwide.
- Degree of foresight: reckless confidence, deliberate risk, or concealed knowledge of likely failure.
- Present aim: survival alone, recovering control, undoing damage, or protecting remaining survivors.
- Visual era and tone: recommended starting direction is industrial electrical science fiction, grounded and weathered, with restrained theatrical features.
